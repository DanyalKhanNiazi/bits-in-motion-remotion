The narration answers it in P06 and P08, then answers it out loud, as "our question", in P22. **Alternative titles**
1. Why AI Reads Your Question at Once but Writes One Piece at a Time

**Viewer promise:** Follow one question from the moment you hit enter to the last piece of the reply, and see why a language model reads your whole prompt in one run but writes its answer one run per piece, with gigabytes of numbers crossing from memory to the math units every time.

**Selected angle: "reads at once, writes one piece at a time", measured; then the bridge; then the three waits you've felt.**

## B. Final voiceover script

The clean copy is also saved as `script_voiceover.txt`. Numbers, model names and acronyms are written the way they should be spoken: three hundredths, Llama three point one, H one hundred, R T X fifty-ninety, V-RAM.

```text
In one of Nvidia's own tests, a small AI model on a single data-center GPU read a prompt about seven hundred and fifty words long in less time than a blink. Under three hundredths of a second. Then it wrote an answer of the same length. That took about six and a half seconds, over two hundred times longer. When a chatbot's answer trickles out a few words at a time, it isn't just a typing effect. Each piece depends on the ones before it, and to write each piece, that model hauls almost sixteen gigabytes of numbers to where the math happens. Almost sixteen gigabytes, for one piece.

Let's follow one question through it all: "Why do AI answers arrive one piece at a time?" And along the way, there's an experiment where researchers capped their chips at half power. One part of the job clearly slowed down. The other barely noticed.

First, the model doesn't read words the way you do. A tokenizer chops text into pieces called tokens and swaps each piece for an ID number from a fixed list, the model's vocabulary. With OpenAI's open-source tokenizer, our ten words and a question mark come out as eleven numbers. Meta's Llama, the open model we'll follow later, cuts them into the same eleven pieces.

Here, every word happened to be one piece, but that isn't the rule. "Photosynthesis" becomes two: "photos," and then "synthesis" without its first letter. For English, OpenAI's rule of thumb is about four characters per token, roughly three-quarters of a word.

Now the model's job. Given all the tokens so far, it scores how likely each token in its vocabulary is to come next. For OpenAI's tokenizer, that's about two hundred thousand options. Then one gets picked, usually a likely one with a dash of randomness. That's why asking the same question twice can get you a different answer.

The chosen token is tacked onto the end, and the model runs again to pick the next one. That's the loop. It normally keeps going until the model picks a special "end" token, which you never see. Without any tricks, a hundred-token answer takes a hundred runs of the model, one after another. Mainstream chatbots write this way, though a few models, mostly experimental, work differently. And later, we'll see a trick that bends the rule.

When an app streams an answer live, you're mostly watching that loop as it runs. Streaming doesn't make the answer finish sooner. A ten-second answer takes ten seconds either way, but you can read the first half while the second half is still being written. Some services send tokens in small bunches. But for most answers, when the first words reach you, the end doesn't exist yet.

Now rewind to the moment you hit enter. Reading your question skips that one-at-a-time loop. When the model writes, it can't start on piece five until it has picked piece four. Your prompt has no such wait: every piece of it is already there. So the model can read all of its tokens side by side in a single run, or chunk by chunk if the prompt is very long. That same run picks the first piece of the answer. Writing the rest is the opposite: one token per run, in order.

So the loop explains the order, and why writing takes so many runs. But why is each writing run so slow? In that same test, reading the whole prompt, about a thousand tokens, took about as long as writing four tokens of the answer. Reading it took roughly a thousand times the math of writing one token, yet only about four times the wait. So the math isn't the slow part.

To pick each token, the model needs its weights, also called parameters: the billions of numbers it learned in training. The companies behind ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini don't say how big those chatbots' models are, so let's use the open model from that test: Meta's Llama three point one, the version with eight billion weights. Each weight is stored as a sixteen-bit number, which takes two bytes. Eight billion weights, two bytes each: about sixteen gigabytes. There's our sixteen gigabytes from the start.

On a GPU, those weights sit in the card's own memory, what gamers call V-RAM, because they're far too big for the tiny storage on the chip itself. But the math happens on the chip. So here's the picture we'll use from now on: a bridge between that memory and the chip's math units. The weights have to cross it to reach the math. The model is a stack of thirty-two layers, each holding its own share of the weights, and every new token has to pass through all of them. So for a single conversation, almost all sixteen gigabytes cross that bridge for every token.

On the Nvidia H one hundred from that test, the bridge itself is fast. It's rated at over three trillion bytes a second. It's only narrow next to the math units, which are faster still. On paper, delivering the weights takes roughly three hundred times longer than the math. If the math took one second, the delivery would take about five minutes. So for one user, the math units spend most of their time waiting. Nvidia's test measured about a hundred and fifty tokens a second. That's faster than you can read, and already about three-quarters of the bridge's theoretical limit.

Here's that experiment. If writing is really waiting on the bridge, slowing the math shouldn't matter much. Researchers ran big open models on an eight-GPU server, packed with as many requests as it could hold. Then they capped every GPU at half power, and less power means slower math. Reading prompts slowed down substantially. Writing answers barely changed. When the model reads a prompt, every number that crosses the bridge gets used for all of the prompt's tokens at once, so the math stays busy. When it writes, each number makes just one new token per conversation, and even a packed server only holds so many conversations at once. Writing was waiting on memory, not math.

For writing, then, a chip's memory bandwidth, how many gigabytes per second can cross that bridge, matters at least as much as its math. Nvidia's R T X fifty-ninety, a top gaming card, has a little over half that H one hundred's bandwidth. In theory, the same model would write at roughly half the speed on it. That's still plenty for one person at home.

With our small model, one GPU writing for one person outruns your reading. Why, then, does a real chatbot sometimes keep you waiting? There are three waits you've probably felt: before the first word, as a chat gets long, and when a service gets busy.

The first wait comes before the first word. Your question has to travel to a data center, and on a busy server, it may wait its turn. Models that show a "Thinking" message also write reasoning tokens first, usually hidden or summarized. On top of that, the big services' models can be much bigger than our example, with more numbers to cross for every token. Anthropic says its models are split across tens of chips or more. More chips means more bridges, but those chips also have to keep passing results to each other.

The second wait can creep up as a chat gets longer. To avoid redoing work, the model keeps running notes on every earlier token: the numbers it already worked out for them. Those notes save math, not travel. Every new token still carries all of them back across the bridge. And in a real chat, the prompt usually includes the whole conversation so far, not just your latest message. For our Llama model, the notes take about a gigabyte for every eight thousand tokens, and every conversation gets its own. The longer the chat, the more there is to haul for every new piece.

The third wait comes from sharing. Big services serve many people at once, and one trip across the bridge can produce the next token for every conversation in a group, which engineers call a batch. On a busy server, our answer is just one of them. Back in Nvidia's benchmark, take a version of the model stored in eight bits per weight instead of sixteen, with short prompts. Serving two hundred requests at once produced about sixty times as many tokens a second as serving just one. But each person's tokens came almost three times further apart. That's the trade-off you can feel. Bigger batches make each token cheaper, but they can make your own stream noticeably slower.

Most of the tricks for making writing faster or cheaper do one of two things: carry fewer bytes per token, or get more tokens out of each trip. Batching is the second kind. That eight-bit version is the first. Store each weight in eight or even four bits instead of sixteen, and there's less to carry across, though squeezing too hard can cost accuracy. In Nvidia's single-user test, eight bits made the writing more than forty percent faster, not twice as fast, partly because moving the weights isn't the only work in each run.

Another way to get more out of each trip is called speculative decoding, and it's the trick that bends the one-at-a-time rule. A small, fast model guesses several tokens ahead. The big model then checks all the guesses in one run, the way it reads a prompt, so one trip across the bridge can deliver several pieces of our answer instead of one. It keeps every guess up to the first one it disagrees with, then adds a token of its own. When the guesses are good, Google's original paper got answers two to three times faster, one request at a time, without changing what the big model is likely to say.

Every one of these tricks changes how long each run takes, or how many pieces one run can deliver. None of them removes the one-after-another loop.

So, back to our question: why do AI answers arrive one piece at a time? Because each piece really does depend on the ones before it, so the pieces have to be made in order. And for a single conversation on a GPU, most of the wait between them isn't the math. It's the trip. Our question went in almost all at once, and its answer comes out exactly the way it describes. So next time an answer trickles onto your screen, you're not just watching a machine type. You're watching gigabytes of numbers cross a bridge, again and again, piece after piece.
```

**Why the final opening differs.** H-B won on its paradox: fast reading against slow writing introduces the episode's core mechanism in four sayable sentences, and it maps straight onto a split-screen animation. The judge flagged two problems with H-B and one with the draft around it:
- "It isn't typing for effect" and "can only happen in order" were unhedged.
- "Every step" was undefined.
- v1's 40-word disclaimer landed inside the first 30 seconds.

The opening then changed in four steps:
1. **v2 (round-1 judge).** v2 took the judge's rewrite almost word for word. It kept H-B's paradox, added H-A's hedges ("It isn't just a typing effect", "In a standard language model") and replaced "every step" with "moving gigabytes of numbers to where the math happens". The disclaimer moved out of the opening and became a specific statement in P10: "The companies behind ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini don't say how big those chatbots' models are."
2. **v3 and v4 (round-2 and round-4 fact-checks).**

The companies behind ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini don't say how big those chatbots' models are, so let's use the open model from that test: Meta's Llama three point one, the version with eight billion weights. Each weight is stored as a sixteen-bit number, which takes two bytes. Eight billion weights, two bytes each: about sixteen gigabytes. There's our sixteen gigabytes from the start.

*Estimated start 4:17 (at 145 wpm) · 83 words · Claims: C35, C36, C37, C38 · Visual: V5*

#### P11 - The bridge (metaphor introduced)
> On a GPU, those weights sit in the card's own memory, what gamers call V-RAM, because they're far too big for the tiny storage on the chip itself. But the math happens on the chip. So here's the picture we'll use from now on: a bridge between that memory and the chip's math units. The weights have to cross it to reach the math. The model is a stack of thirty-two layers, each holding its own share of the weights, and every new token has to pass through all of them. So for a single conversation, almost all sixteen gigabytes cross that bridge for every token.

*Estimated start 4:51 (at 145 wpm) · 106 words · Claims: C39, C40, C41, C42 · Visual: V5*

#### P12 - A fast bridge, faster math
> On the Nvidia H one hundred from that test, the bridge itself is fast. It's rated at over three trillion bytes a second. It's only narrow next to the math units, which are faster still. On paper, delivering the weights takes roughly three hundred times longer than the math. If the math took one second, the delivery would take about five minutes. So for one user, the math units spend most of their time waiting. Nvidia's test measured about a hundred and fifty tokens a second. That's faster than you can read, and already about three-quarters of the bridge's theoretical limit.

*Estimated start 5:35 (at 145 wpm) · 101 words · Claims: C43, C44, C45, C46, C47, C48, C89 · Visual: V5*

#### P13 - The half-power experiment
> Here's that experiment. If writing is really waiting on the bridge, slowing the math shouldn't matter much. Researchers ran big open models on an eight-GPU server, packed with as many requests as it could hold. Then they capped every GPU at half power, and less power means slower math. Reading prompts slowed down substantially. Writing answers barely changed. When the model reads a prompt, every number that crosses the bridge gets used for all of the prompt's tokens at once, so the math stays busy. When it writes, each number makes just one new token per conversation, and even a packed server only holds so many conversations at once. Writing was waiting on memory, not math.

*Estimated start 6:17 (at 145 wpm) · 116 words · Claims: C49, C50, C51, C52, C53, C54, C93 · Visual: V6*

#### P14 - Bandwidth on a gaming card
> For writing, then, a chip's memory bandwidth, how many gigabytes per second can cross that bridge, matters at least as much as its math. Nvidia's R T X fifty-ninety, a top gaming card, has a little over half that H one hundred's bandwidth. In theory, the same model would write at roughly half the speed on it. That's still plenty for one person at home.

*Estimated start 7:05 (at 145 wpm) · 65 words · Claims: C55, C56, C57 · Visual: V7*

#### P15 - Three waits you've felt
> With our small model, one GPU writing for one person outruns your reading. Why, then, does a real chatbot sometimes keep you waiting? There are three waits you've probably felt: before the first word, as a chat gets long, and when a service gets busy.

*Estimated start 7:32 (at 145 wpm) · 41 words · Claims: C90, C91 · Visual: V8*

#### P16 - Wait one: before the first word
> The first wait comes before the first word. Your question has to travel to a data center, and on a busy server, it may wait its turn. Models that show a "Thinking" message also write reasoning tokens first, usually hidden or summarized. On top of that, the big services' models can be much bigger than our example, with more numbers to cross for every token. Anthropic says its models are split across tens of chips or more. More chips means more bridges, but those chips also have to keep passing results to each other.

*Estimated start 7:49 (at 145 wpm) · 94 words · Claims: C63, C64, C65, C66, C67 · Visual: V8*

#### P17 - Wait two: long chats and running notes
> The second wait can creep up as a chat gets longer. To avoid redoing work, the model keeps running notes on every earlier token: the numbers it already worked out for them. Those notes save math, not travel. Every new token still carries all of them back across the bridge. And in a real chat, the prompt usually includes the whole conversation so far, not just your latest message. For our Llama model, the notes take about a gigabyte for every eight thousand tokens, and every conversation gets its own. The longer the chat, the more there is to haul for every new piece.

*Estimated start 8:28 (at 145 wpm) · 104 words · Claims: C58, C59, C60, C61, C62 · Visual: V9*

#### P18 - Wait three: sharing the trip
> The third wait comes from sharing. Big services serve many people at once, and one trip across the bridge can produce the next token for every conversation in a group, which engineers call a batch. On a busy server, our answer is just one of them. Back in Nvidia's benchmark, take a version of the model stored in eight bits per weight instead of sixteen, with short prompts. Serving two hundred requests at once produced about sixty times as many tokens a second as serving just one. But each person's tokens came almost three times further apart. That's the trade-off you can feel. Bigger batches make each token cheaper, but they can make your own stream noticeably slower.

*Estimated start 9:11 (at 145 wpm) · 118 words · Claims: C68, C70, C73, C74, C75, C76, C78 · Visual: V10*

#### P19 - Fewer bytes per trip
> Most of the tricks for making writing faster or cheaper do one of two things: carry fewer bytes per token, or get more tokens out of each trip. Batching is the second kind. That eight-bit version is the first. Store each weight in eight or even four bits instead of sixteen, and there's less to carry across, though squeezing too hard can cost accuracy. In Nvidia's single-user test, eight bits made the writing more than forty percent faster, not twice as fast, partly because moving the weights isn't the only work in each run.

*Estimated start 10:00 (at 145 wpm) · 94 words · Claims: C69, C71, C72 · Visual: V10*

#### P20 - Guessing ahead
> Another way to get more out of each trip is called speculative decoding, and it's the trick that bends the one-at-a-time rule. A small, fast model guesses several tokens ahead. The big model then checks all the guesses in one run, the way it reads a prompt, so one trip across the bridge can deliver several pieces of our answer instead of one. It keeps every guess up to the first one it disagrees with, then adds a token of its own. When the guesses are good, Google's original paper got answers two to three times faster, one request at a time, without changing what the big model is likely to say.

*Estimated start 10:38 (at 145 wpm) · 112 words · Claims: C21, C80, C81, C82 · Visual: V11*

#### P21 - No trick removes the loop
> Every one of these tricks changes how long each run takes, or how many pieces one run can deliver. None of them removes the one-after-another loop.

*Estimated start 11:25 (at 145 wpm) · 26 words · Claims: C84 · Visual: V12*

#### P22 - Back to our question
> So, back to our question: why do AI answers arrive one piece at a time? Because each piece really does depend on the ones before it, so the pieces have to be made in order. And for a single conversation on a GPU, most of the wait between them isn't the math. It's the trip. Our question went in almost all at once, and its answer comes out exactly the way it describes. So next time an answer trickles onto your screen, you're not just watching a machine type. You're watching gigabytes of numbers cross a bridge, again and again, piece after piece.

*Estimated start 11:36 (at 145 wpm) · 103 words · Claims: C85, C86, C87, C92 · Visual: V12*

### Unresolved research limitations

None of these reverses a narrated claim; each is why a qualifier sits in the narration.
1. **No first-party data on how any chatbot app paces live text or splits its first-word delay** (C01, C22, C24, C25, C63, C87). Hedged with "isn't just", "mostly", "Some services", "may wait its turn".
2. **Chatbot model sizes and layouts are undisclosed** (C36, C65, C66, C68). For Anthropic the support is the absence of a published size plus its "tens of chips or more" footnote. "Can be much bigger" also covers mixture-of-experts models.
3. **Time to first token is not pure reading time** (C02, C32, C33, C88). It includes the first answer token and overhead, so it is an upper bound on reading; "less than a blink" and "about four tokens" only get truer. 750 words converts 1,000 tokens with OpenAI's English rule of thumb.
4. **On-paper ratios use peak spec-sheet rates for the H100 SXM** (C43, C44, C48, C56, C57, C86, C89). The NVL part would give about 214× and put the RTX 5090 a little under half. Narration says "rated", "On paper", "theoretical", "In theory".
5. **Per-token traffic is about 15 GB, not 16** (C06, C38, C42): a round-up of about 5%.
6. **The RTX 5090 speed is a bandwidth limit, not a benchmark** (C57); downloading Llama requires accepting Meta's licence (C03).
7. **The half-power experiment is a different setup, and the prompt-phase figure at 350 W is only plotted** (C07, C49–C51). Keep the setup card on screen.
8. **Nvidia doesn't explain the 1.44× eight-bit gain** (C72); "partly because" is an inference.
9. **Batching numbers come from one row of one benchmark** (C70, C75, C76, C78); the condition "with short prompts" is spoken. Longer prompts gained much less (about 16× and 7×), which the narration no longer mentions; the condition keeps the claim true.
10. **The speculative-decoding speed-up is an older, specific result** (C82): T5-XXL, batch 1, TPU-v4; "original" follows Google's framing.
11. **No first-party statement that each named chatbot is autoregressive** (C20, C84, C85); the narration names the exception.
12. **The bridge picture is specific to HBM/GDDR-fed accelerators** (C40, C86, C87, C89); it may not describe wafer-scale tiers. The close is scoped "for a single conversation on a GPU" and "not just".
13. **Simplifications kept on purpose** (C28, C40, C52, C58, C60, C61, C67, C92): "already there" includes earlier turns; "running notes" stands for the KV cache; "the weights cross" leaves out on-chip caching; notes' size is specific to the 8B at 16-bit; "the long chat wait can creep up" is modest for this model (about +4 GB at 32,000 tokens, C62).
